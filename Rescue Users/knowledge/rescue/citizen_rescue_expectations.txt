==============================================================================
FLOOD RESCUE SYSTEM - CITIZEN RESCUE EXPECTATIONS & SURVIVAL PROTOCOLS
What Every Citizen Must Know About Rescue Timelines, Boat Capacity & Survival
==============================================================================

1. REALISTIC RESCUE ARRIVAL TIMELINES (RESCUE IS DISPATCHED, NOT INSTANT)
When you press the SOS button in the FloodGuard app or call 112/108, your distress signal and GPS coordinates are registered immediately on the central rescue dispatch dashboard. However, physical rescue teams CANNOT appear instantly within a few seconds or minutes. Rescue personnel (NDRF, SDRF, Fire & Rescue Services) must assemble equipment, inflate motorized rescue boats, navigate across washed-out roads, bypass submerged roadblocks, and fight powerful water currents. In urban and easily accessible sectors, typical rescue boat arrival times range between 15 to 45 minutes. In severely cut-off, peripheral, or rural areas, arrival times can extend from 2 to 4 hours or longer. Stay calm, secure high ground, and prepare for extraction rather than panicking.

2. RESCUE BOAT CAPACITY LIMITS: PARTIAL BATCH EXTRACTIONS, NOT TOTAL SIMULTANEOUS EVACUATION
Inflatable Rescue Boats (IRBs) and disaster motorboats have strict payload capacities, typically certified to safely carry only 6 to 10 persons per trip depending on current velocity and water turbulence. Overloaded boats will capsize in turbulent water, endangering both victims and rescuers. Therefore, rescue teams arrive to extract people in prioritized batches ("that much only, not the total group at once"). Rescuers cannot evacuate an entire apartment building, school, or residential lane in a single pass. Families must understand that rescue teams will shuttle back and forth over multiple runs. Cooperate fully with the boat commander when they designate who boards the current run.

3. TRIAGE & EXTRACTION PRIORITY HIERARCHY
Rescuers operate under strict disaster triage protocols to maximize lives saved. The extraction order is:
- Priority 1 (Immediate Danger): Individuals trapped in rapidly collapsing structures, submerged vehicles, people stranded in open rising water, individuals with acute medical emergencies, injured persons, pregnant women in active labor, and unaccompanied infants.
- Priority 2 (High Vulnerability): Elderly individuals, nursing mothers with young children, disabled persons, and individuals with chronic medical dependencies (e.g. dialysis, oxygen) currently in stable dry shelter.
- Priority 3 (Stable High Ground): Able-bodied adults sheltering on safe, high concrete rooftops with access to dry shelter and drinking water. If you are safe on a concrete roof and water is not rising toward your floor, the rescue boat will prioritize people in life-threatening positions before returning for you.

4. WHAT CITIZENS MUST LEAVE BEHIND (BAGGAGE RESTRICTIONS)
During flood evacuations, space and buoyancy on rescue boats are strictly reserved for human lives. Rescue personnel are instructed to strictly refuse luggage, rolling suitcases, duffel bags, televisions, household electronics, heavy blankets, or furniture. Attempting to bring large baggage slows down the extraction and reduces the number of people who can be saved on each run. Every individual is permitted ONLY one small, lightweight, waterproof bag or pouch containing:
- Life-saving prescription medications (insulin, heart pills, asthma inhalers).
- Essential identity documents, deeds, and passports sealed in airtight ziploc bags.
- Mobile phone and a fully charged compact power bank.
- Emergency cash and keys.
- Small quantity of baby formula / infant supplies if travelling with an infant.

5. HOW TO SIGNAL RESCUE TEAMS EFFECTIVELY WHILE WAITING
Motorized rescue boats and low-flying disaster helicopters operate in high-noise environments where human screaming cannot be heard over engine roar and rushing water. Use standard signaling methods:
- Audio Distress Signal: Use a sports or safety whistle. Blow three distinct, sharp blasts (blast - blast - blast), pause for 5 seconds, and repeat. Three blasts is the universal distress signal and cuts through wind and engine noise.
- Daytime Visual Signal: Wave a brightly colored cloth, towel, or bedsheet (preferably red, bright orange, or fluorescent yellow) tied to a stick or broom handle from the highest visible point of the roof.
- Nighttime Visual Signal: Flash your phone light or LED torch toward the rescuers in sets of three pulses (light - light - light), then pause. Do NOT shine continuous high-beam light directly into the boat pilot's eyes as this impairs their night vision and water navigation.
- Ground-to-Air Signal: If disaster helicopters are overhead, lay out clothing or bedsheets forming a large letter "V" (Requiring Assistance) or "X" (Requiring Medical Assistance) on an open, uncluttered roof surface.

6. SAFETY RULES WHEN BOARDING AND RIDING RESCUE CRAFT
When a rescue boat maneuvers alongside your building or rooftop:
- Do not rush, push, or lunge toward the craft. The wash from the boat's propeller or sudden movement can cause victims to slip into deep water or tip the craft.
- Follow the exact sequence directed by the boat commander. Allow infants, children, and elderly persons to be handed across first with the support of rescue swimmers.
- Step directly into the center line of the boat, keeping your body low. Never step onto the inflated rubber gunwales.
- Immediately put on a personal flotation device (lifejacket) if handed one by the crew.
- Remain seated flat on the boat floor or designated bench throughout transit. Keep your hands, arms, and legs inside the boat perimeter to prevent crushing against floating debris or submerged posts.
- Maintain silence and do not stand up or wave during transit, so the boat pilot can hear water depth warnings and engine commands.
